What the game actually is

Strip away the tournaments and the trophies, and hockey is a simple idea: two teams, one ball, and a stick each, trying to send the ball into a goal more times than the other side.

What most of the world calls "hockey" — and what is officially called field hockey to tell it apart from its cousin on ice — is played on a rectangular pitch of grass or artificial turf, with eleven players per side, including a goalkeeper. Each outfield player carries a stick that is flat on one face and rounded on the other; only the flat face is allowed to touch the ball, which is what gives the game its particular rhythm of turned wrists and reversed strokes.

It is one of the oldest team sports still played in a form its ancient originators would recognise. Long before it had rules written down anywhere, people across very different parts of the world had already worked out the same basic idea — a stick, a ball, and a goal to defend.

A game older than most

Tomb paintings from Beni Hasan in Egypt, roughly four thousand years old, show two figures with curved sticks and a ball between them. Ancient Greek carvings show much the same scene, as do old Persian and Ethiopian accounts of stick games played on open ground. The idea clearly occurred to a lot of people independently, long before anyone thought to write down how it should be played.

The version played today took shape in England in the nineteenth century. Clubs began forming in the 1850s and 60s, and in 1886 several of them came together in London to form the Hockey Association, which set down a common rulebook for the first time. From England, the game travelled with soldiers, schools, and traders to every corner of the British Empire — and in more than one of those places, it was taken up with a seriousness that would eventually outgrow the country that introduced it.

The years India owned the podium

No country has ever dominated an Olympic team sport the way India dominated hockey in the middle of the twentieth century. The team didn't just win — for three decades, it was barely beaten.

India played its first Olympic hockey match in 1928, in Amsterdam, having only formed a national federation three years earlier. The team won every match it played and took the gold medal on debut, with a young forward named Dhyan Chand scoring goal after goal, including a hat-trick in the final. It was the beginning of a run that nobody has come close to repeating since: six straight gold medals from 1928 to 1956, and eight out of nine Olympics won between 1928 and 1980.

Dhyan Chand himself became the game's first true global star, so precise with the ball that opposing officials reportedly once broke his stick mid-match to check it wasn't hiding a magnet. There wasn't one — just a level of stick control that hasn't dated in the ninety years since.

How a match actually works

The rules have changed shape a few times over the decades — most notably, the old offside rule was dropped entirely in the early 1990s — but the core of the game has stayed put.

The pitch

A rectangle of grass or artificial turf about 91 metres long and 55 metres wide, with a goal at each end and a curved scoring zone, known as "the D," in front of each one.

The teams

Eleven players a side on the pitch at once, one of them a goalkeeper, with substitutes rotating in and out through the match far more freely than in most team sports.

The time

International matches run for four quarters of 15 minutes each, with short breaks between them — a format introduced to give broadcasters and tired legs alike a breather.

Scoring

A goal only counts if the ball is played from inside the D. Anything struck from outside that zone, however good it looks, doesn't count — which shapes almost every attacking move in the game.

Touching the ball

Only the flat face of the stick may touch the ball. Kicking it, or using the rounded back edge of the stick, is a foul — except for the goalkeeper inside their own circle.

Penalty corners

Awarded for fouls by the defending side inside the D, or deliberate fouls just outside it — a set-piece moment that has decided more finals than almost anything else in the game.

Who plays where

Formations shift from team to team, but every side is built around the same four jobs, laid out here the way they'd sit on the pitch — goal to goal.

Goalkeeper

The only player allowed to kick the ball or block it with any part of the body, but only inside the shooting circle.

Defenders

Fullbacks who mark attackers, break up moves, and start attacks with long, accurate passes out of defence.

Midfielders

Often called "halves" — the ones who cover the most ground, linking defence to attack and back again all match long.

Forwards

Strikers and wingers whose job is to get into the D and turn possession into goals.

The stick and the ball

A hockey stick looks deceptively simple — wood or, more often now, a carbon and fibreglass composite, with a long handle and a short, hooked head. The trick is that it's curved on one face only. That single rule shapes everything about how the game is played, forcing players to constantly turn the stick, reverse their stance, or switch hands rather than simply swinging at the ball from any angle.

The ball itself is hard plastic, about the size of a cricket ball, with a dimpled surface a little like a golf ball's, designed to keep it stable at speed on turf. Hit properly, it can travel well over 100 kilometres an hour — fast enough that goalkeepers wear a full set of padding, a helmet, and a chest guard just to stand in front of it.

One game, several forms

Take the same basic idea — a stick, a ball, two goals — and move it to a different surface, and you get a whole family of related games. Ice hockey developed in the cold winters of Canada and northern Europe, where players simply took stick-and-ball games onto frozen ponds and swapped the ball for a puck that would behave on ice. Indoor hockey shrinks the field-hockey pitch down for a smaller, faster five-a-side game played on a hard court. And at grass-roots level, from school playgrounds to university leagues, the game is played and re-played by people who will never see an Olympic pitch, which is really where most of its history has actually happened.

Why it's stayed worth watching

Hockey rewards a strange mix of skills that don't usually sit together — the patience to build a passing move over thirty seconds, and the speed to finish it in half of one. A stick that limits how you can touch the ball, rather than making things easier, is what forces the close control and quick-wristed passing that make a good match so watchable. It's a sport that's been a school playground game and an Olympic final at the same time for well over a century, and both versions are, underneath it all, the same game.

None of this page is selling anything — it's simply an introduction to a sport that's been played, in one form or another, for longer than almost any other team game still on the field today.
